# Theorem Bank: Probability, Symmetry, and Homological Probability

This draft treats the transcript as a research-program source. Statements marked **Theorem** are standard, tautological under the definitions, or mathematically safe under the stated hypotheses. Statements marked **Conjecture** or **Program theorem** capture the proposed direction without pretending that it is already proved.

## 0. Meta-principle: mathematics as correct naming

### Principle 0.1 — Poincaré–Grothendieck naming principle

**Statement.** A mathematical theory becomes powerful when it identifies several apparently different phenomena as instances of the same structure and gives that structure the correct name.

**Robust content.** The relevant object is not a formula in isolation, but the invariant pattern preserved across examples.

**Typical forms.**

* A probability model becomes robust when it is functorial under changes of observables.
* A geometric model becomes robust when it is stable under perturbation, scaling, or passage to a limit.
* A biological or linguistic model becomes mathematical when it identifies the invariant combinatorial, categorical, or topological structure behind observed data.

## 1. Classical probability as the first layer

### Theorem 1.1 — Hardy–Weinberg idempotence

**Statement.** Let (A=(a_{ij})) be a nonnegative matrix with

[
\sum_{i,j} a_{ij}=1.
]

Define row and column marginals

[
r_i=\sum_j a_{ij}, \qquad c_j=\sum_i a_{ij}.
]

Define the recombination map

[
T(A)_{ij}=r_i c_j.
]

Then

[
T^2=T.
]

Equivalently, after one random-mating step, the population lies on the rank-one marginal product locus, and further random mating does not change the genotype distribution.

**Robust content.** Mendelian inheritance is not merely a numerical formula. It is an idempotent rational transformation on a simplex of genotype distributions.

**Proof sketch.** The row and column marginals of (T(A)) are again (r_i) and (c_j). Therefore applying (T) again gives the same matrix.

---

### Theorem 1.2 — Law of large numbers as state stabilization

**Statement.** Let (X_1,X_2,\dots) be independent identically distributed random variables with finite expectation. Then

[
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N X_i \to \mathbb E[X_1]
]

in probability, and under standard hypotheses almost surely.

**Robust content.** Probability becomes physically meaningful not because individual microscopic states have observable probability, but because aggregate statistics stabilize.

---

### Theorem 1.3 — Entropy as asymptotic state counting

**Statement.** Let (p=(p_1,\dots,p_k)) be a probability vector. The number of length-(N) words with empirical frequencies approximately (p_i) grows as

[
\exp\bigl(NH(p)+o(N)\bigr),
]

where

[
H(p)=-\sum_i p_i \log p_i.
]

**Robust content.** Shannon entropy is the logarithmic growth rate of the number of effectively observable states.

**Interpretation.** The slogan “entropy is the logarithm of the number of states” becomes correct only after passing to repeated experiments and using concentration.

---

### Theorem 1.4 — Subadditivity of entropy

**Statement.** For two finite random variables (X,Y),

[
H(X,Y)\le H(X)+H(Y),
]

with equality precisely when (X) and (Y) are independent.

More generally,

[
H(X,Y,Z)+H(Y)\le H(X,Y)+H(Y,Z),
]

which is strong subadditivity in the classical case.

**Robust content.** Observation reduces uncertainty. Correlation decreases the number of independently observable states.

---

### Theorem 1.5 — Fisher geometry of the simplex

**Statement.** On the interior of the probability simplex

[
\Delta^{n-1}={p_i>0,\ \sum_i p_i=1},
]

the Hessian of negative Shannon entropy gives the Fisher metric

[
g=\sum_i \frac{dp_i^2}{p_i}.
]

Under the square-root map

[
p\mapsto 2(\sqrt{p_1},\dots,\sqrt{p_n}),
]

this metric becomes the standard spherical metric on the positive orthant of a sphere.

**Robust content.** Entropy secretly contains a constant-curvature Riemannian geometry.

## 2. Kinetic probability and functoriality

### Program theorem 2.1 — Boltzmann naturality principle

**Statement.** A kinetic equation should not be viewed merely as a differential equation attached to one gas. It should be viewed as a natural transformation from a category of microscopic collision systems to a category of macroscopic evolution equations.

Schematically,

[
\text{Microscopic collision law}
\longmapsto
\text{kinetic equation}
\longmapsto
\text{hydrodynamic equation}.
]

**Robust content.** The physically meaningful structure is not a single formula but the functorial passage from one level of description to another.

**Caution.** This is a theorem schema, not a finished theorem, until the relevant categories, morphisms, and limiting procedures are specified.

---

### Theorem 2.2 — BBGKY hierarchy

**Statement.** Let (f_N) be the (N)-particle distribution evolving under Liouville dynamics. Let (f_N^{(k)}) be the (k)-particle marginal. Then the marginals satisfy a coupled hierarchy of the form

[
\partial_t f_N^{(k)} + L_k f_N^{(k)}
====================================

C_{k,k+1} f_N^{(k+1)}.
]

Thus the evolution of (k)-particle statistics depends on ((k+1))-particle statistics.

**Robust content.** Marginalization is not closed. Closure requires an additional assumption, typically some form of independence or propagation of chaos.

---

### Theorem 2.3 — Boltzmann equation as a short-time limit

**Statement.** In the Boltzmann–Grad scaling limit, and under suitable chaotic initial data, hard-sphere particle dynamics converges for short times to the Boltzmann hierarchy; factorized solutions yield the Boltzmann equation.

**Robust content.** The Boltzmann equation is not merely formal. It is rigorously derivable in a restricted scaling regime, but the physically relevant long-time regime remains much subtler.

---

### Program theorem 2.4 — Entropy-compatible closure principle

**Statement.** A hydrodynamic closure of a kinetic hierarchy is physically admissible only if it is compatible with the monotonicity of entropy.

**Robust content.** Formal asymptotic expansion is not enough. The correct reduced equation must preserve the thermodynamic direction of time.

## 3. Percolation and topology

### Theorem 3.1 — Branching-process threshold on a regular tree

**Statement.** Consider bond percolation on a rooted (d)-ary tree, where each edge is retained independently with probability (p). There is a positive probability of an infinite open path if and only if

[
dp>1.
]

Therefore

[
p_c=\frac1d.
]

For the binary tree, (p_c=1/2).

**Robust content.** Percolation on a tree is exactly a Galton–Watson branching process.

---

### Theorem 3.2 — Square-lattice bond percolation threshold

**Statement.** For independent bond percolation on the square lattice (\mathbb Z^2),

[
p_c=\frac12.
]

**Robust content.** The threshold is governed by planar duality, but the full theorem requires nontrivial probability estimates beyond the visual duality argument.

---

### Theorem 3.3 — Smirnov–Cardy conformal invariance

**Statement.** Critical site percolation on the triangular lattice has conformally invariant scaling limits for crossing probabilities. In particular, crossing probabilities converge to Cardy’s formula.

For an equilateral triangle, the limiting probability that a critical cluster connects the relevant boundary arcs is linear in the boundary coordinate.

**Robust content.** At criticality, a discrete probabilistic model produces a conformal invariant of planar domains.

---

### Conjecture 3.4 — Homological percolation duality

**Statement.** Let (M) be a closed (d)-manifold with a cell decomposition and dual cell decomposition. In a random subcomplex model, the appearance of (k)-dimensional homological connectivity should be coupled to the disappearance of complementary ((d-k-1))-dimensional dual obstructions.

In a self-dual situation, the critical parameter should often occur at

[
p=\frac12.
]

**Robust content.** Percolation thresholds are not only probabilistic phenomena. They are controlled by Poincaré duality and the topology of random subcomplexes.

**Status.** This is a theorem in some special models, a guiding principle in others.

## 4. Self-avoiding walks

### Theorem 4.1 — Existence of the connective constant

**Statement.** Let (c_n) be the number of (n)-step self-avoiding walks from a fixed vertex in a transitive lattice. Then the limit

[
\mu=\lim_{n\to\infty} c_n^{1/n}
]

exists.

The number (\mu) is called the connective constant.

**Robust content.** Even when the geometry of typical self-avoiding walks is poorly understood, their exponential counting rate is stable.

---

### Theorem 4.2 — Honeycomb-lattice connective constant

**Statement.** For the hexagonal, or honeycomb, lattice,

[
\mu=\sqrt{2+\sqrt2}.
]

**Robust content.** Some self-avoiding walk models possess hidden discrete holomorphic structure strong enough to determine exact constants.

---

### Conjecture 4.3 — Metric exponent for self-avoiding walks

**Statement.** A typical (n)-step self-avoiding walk in (\mathbb R^d) should have diameter of order

[
n^\nu,
]

where (\nu>1/2) for (d=2,3), reflecting the fact that self-avoidance forces the walk to spread more than ordinary Brownian motion.

Predicted values include

[
\nu=\frac34 \quad \text{in } d=2,
]

and approximately

[
\nu\approx 0.588 \quad \text{in } d=3.
]

**Robust content.** The hard problem is not counting walks but proving concentration of their large-scale geometry.

---

### Definition 4.4 — Configuration-space model of self-avoidance

An (n)-step embedded polymer may be modeled as a point in a high-dimensional configuration space

[
{(x_0,\dots,x_n): |x_{i+1}-x_i|=1,\ |x_i-x_j|\ge \varepsilon \text{ for } i\ne j}.
]

This is the complement of thickened diagonals.

**Robust content.** Self-avoiding walk theory is a probability theory on a highly nontrivial configuration space, not merely a random walk with a constraint.

## 5. Protein folding as filtered topology

### Definition 5.1 — Energy landscape filtration

Let (\mathcal C) be a configuration space of molecular conformations, and let

[
E:\mathcal C\to \mathbb R
]

be an energy or free-energy function. Define sublevel spaces

[
\mathcal C_{\le a}={x\in \mathcal C:E(x)\le a}.
]

---

### Theorem 5.2 — Reachability through sublevel connectivity

**Statement.** A conformation (q) is reachable from a conformation (p) without crossing energy level (a) if and only if (p) and (q) lie in the same connected component of (\mathcal C_{\le a}).

**Robust content.** Folding barriers are topological features of an energy filtration.

---

### Program theorem 5.3 — Folding as percolation in configuration space

**Statement.** Protein folding can be modeled as a percolation problem on the filtered configuration space

[
\mathcal C_{\le a}.
]

As the physical parameter (a), temperature, pH, solvent condition, or free energy threshold changes, the topology of accessible conformations changes.

**Robust content.** Folding is not merely minimization of energy. It is controlled by the connectivity and bottlenecks of an energy-filtered configuration space.

**Caution.** This is a conceptual theorem schema. Real proteins require correlated interactions, solvent effects, steric constraints, and sequence-dependent geometry.

## 6. Probability in language

### Definition 6.1 — Context tree of a string

Given a corpus and a finite string (w), define the context tree (T(w)) whose branches record extensions of (w) to the left and right, with edge weights given by empirical frequencies.

The scalar frequency of (w) is only the root weight. The tree records how (w) occurs.

---

### Theorem 6.2 — Scalar frequency is not a linguistic probability invariant

**Statement.** Two strings can have the same empirical frequency but non-isomorphic context trees. Therefore scalar frequency alone cannot distinguish their linguistic behavior.

**Robust content.** In language, probability should often take values in weighted trees or graphs, not only in real numbers.

---

### Theorem 6.3 — Winograd obstruction to syntax-only inference

**Statement.** Suppose two sentences have the same syntactic form but require different pronoun resolutions because of lexical or semantic content. Then any model depending only on syntactic form must assign the same resolution to both and therefore fails on at least one of them.

**Example form.**

[
\text{The object does not fit in the container because it is too large.}
]

versus

[
\text{The object does not fit in the container because it is too small.}
]

**Robust content.** Some linguistic probabilities are semantic-contextual invariants, not syntactic frequencies.

---

### Program theorem 6.4 — Language probability as a functor

**Statement.** A probability theory for language should be a functor

[
\mathsf{Language}\longrightarrow \mathsf{WeightedGraphs}
]

or

[
\mathsf{Language}\longrightarrow \mathsf{ContextTrees},
]

rather than only a map to ([0,1]).

**Robust content.** The probability of a sentence is not merely “how often it appears.” It is the structured distribution of its possible contexts, continuations, and semantic roles.

## 7. Homological probability

### Definition 7.1 — Observable map

Let (X) be a state space and (O) an observable space. An observation is modeled by a map

[
f:X\to O.
]

For an observable event (U\subset O), the corresponding state-space event is

[
X_U=f^{-1}(U).
]

---

### Definition 7.2 — Cohomological support ideal

Let (H^*(X;F)) be cohomology with coefficients in a field (F). Define

[
I(U)=\ker\left(H^*(X;F)\to H^*(X\setminus X_U;F)\right).
]

Thus (I(U)) consists of cohomology classes supported inside the event (X_U).

---

### Theorem 7.3 — Support ideals behave like generalized measures

**Statement.** The assignment

[
U\mapsto I(U)
]

has the following properties.

If (U\subset V), then

[
I(U)\subset I(V).
]

If (U,V\subset O), then

[
I(U)\cdot I(V)\subset I(U\cap V),
]

where the product is the cup product in (H^*(X;F)).

For sufficiently separated or excisive disjoint events, cohomology with support decomposes additively.

**Robust content.** A homological probability does not assign a number to an event. It assigns an ideal, subspace, or algebraic object measuring which global topological features are forced to live over that event.

---

### Definition 7.4 — Homological probability

A homological probability theory is an assignment

[
U\longmapsto I(U)
]

from observable events to algebraic invariants such as subspaces, ideals, modules, or spectra, satisfying monotonicity, functoriality, and multiplicative compatibility.

---

### Theorem 7.5 — Scalar probability as a shadow

**Statement.** Given a homological probability (U\mapsto I(U)), scalar invariants may be obtained by applying rank, dimension, Euler characteristic, Poincaré polynomial, or asymptotic logarithmic growth:

[
U\mapsto \dim I(U),
]

[
U\mapsto P_{I(U)}(t),
]

[
U\mapsto \lim_{N\to\infty}\frac1N\log \dim I_N(U).
]

**Robust content.** Ordinary probability and entropy are numerical shadows of richer functorial structures.

## 8. Cycle spaces and homological spectra

### Theorem 8.1 — Almgren cycle-space theorem

**Statement.** Let (\mathcal Z_k(M;G)) be the space of (k)-cycles in a manifold (M) with coefficients in an abelian group (G). Then, under the standard topology on flat cycles,

[
\pi_i(\mathcal Z_k(M;G))\cong H_{i+k}(M;G).
]

For (M=S^n) and (0<k<n), this gives an Eilenberg–Mac Lane type space with essential homotopy in degree (n-k).

**Robust content.** The topology of the space of cycles is controlled by the homology of the underlying manifold.

---

### Definition 8.2 — Volume filtration on cycle space

Let

[
\mathcal Z_k^{\le V}(M)
=======================

{Z\in \mathcal Z_k(M):\operatorname{Vol}_k(Z)\le V}.
]

The inclusions

[
\mathcal Z_k^{\le V}(M)\hookrightarrow \mathcal Z_k(M)
]

produce a filtration of the cycle space by volume.

---

### Program theorem 8.3 — Homological spectrum of cycles

**Statement.** For a homology or cohomology class (\alpha) of the cycle space, define its spectral value by

[
\lambda(\alpha)=
\inf{V:\alpha \text{ is detected in } \mathcal Z_k^{\le V}(M)}.
]

The collection of all (\lambda(\alpha)) forms a homological spectrum.

**Robust content.** Eigenvalues of operators are only the first example of a more general phenomenon: topology appearing at critical values of a filtration.

---

### Theorem 8.4 — Weyl law for the volume spectrum

**Statement.** For hypersurface sweepouts in a closed Riemannian manifold (M^D), the (p)-th width (\omega_p(M)) satisfies a Weyl-type asymptotic law

[
\omega_p(M)\sim a_D,\operatorname{Vol}(M)^{(D-1)/D}p^{1/D},
]

where (a_D) is a universal constant depending only on the dimension.

**Robust content.** Minimal hypersurface spectra behave like eigenvalue spectra: high-index objects distribute according to volume.

---

### Theorem 8.5 — Rayleigh quotient as topological spectrum

**Statement.** Let (A) be a Hermitian matrix with distinct eigenvalues. The Rayleigh quotient

[
R_A([z])=\frac{\langle Az,z\rangle}{\langle z,z\rangle}
]

on complex projective space (\mathbb CP^{n-1}) is a Morse function. Its critical values are exactly the eigenvalues of (A). The topology of sublevel sets changes precisely at these eigenvalues.

**Robust content.** Linear algebraic spectrum can be recovered as the critical-value spectrum of a topological filtration.

## 9. Waist, packing, and covering

### Theorem 9.1 — Gromov waist theorem for the sphere

**Statement.** Let

[
f:S^n\to \mathbb R^m
]

be continuous. Then there exists a point (y\in \mathbb R^m) such that the (r)-neighborhood of the fiber (f^{-1}(y)) has volume at least the (r)-neighborhood of an equatorial sphere (S^{n-m}\subset S^n), for every (r>0).

**Robust content.** Every map from a sphere to a lower-dimensional space has at least one large fiber.

---

### Conjecture 9.2 — Hausdorff waist sharpening

**Statement.** Under suitable regularity assumptions, one expects a fiber satisfying

[
\mathcal H^{n-m}(f^{-1}(y))
\ge
\operatorname{Vol}(S^{n-m}).
]

**Robust content.** The known waist theorem controls neighborhoods, or Minkowski content. A direct Hausdorff-measure version is subtler.

---

### Program theorem 9.3 — Parametric packing principle

**Statement.** Let (C_n^r(M)) be the configuration space of (n) disjoint radius-(r) balls in a metric manifold (M). Homology classes in (C_n^r(M)) detect not only whether balls fit, but how they can move.

**Robust content.** Packing is not merely a scalar optimization problem. It has a homological refinement: the topology of the configuration space records movable packings.

## 10. Curvature and sweepouts

### Theorem 10.1 — Scalar curvature width bound for (S^3)

**Statement.** Let (g) be a Riemannian metric on (S^3) with scalar curvature

[
R_g\ge 6.
]

Then the Simon–Smith width satisfies

[
W(S^3,g)\le 4\pi.
]

Equivalently, there exists a sweepout of (S^3) by two-spheres whose maximal area is at most (4\pi).

**Robust content.** Positive scalar curvature imposes a global upper bound on certain two-dimensional sweepout areas.

---

### Conjecture 10.2 — Higher-dimensional scalar-curvature homological spectrum

**Statement.** Let (M^n) carry a metric satisfying

[
R_g\ge n(n-1).
]

Then the homological spectra of suitable cycle spaces of (M) should satisfy universal upper bounds analogous to the round sphere.

**Robust content.** Scalar curvature should constrain not only individual minimal hypersurfaces but the entire homological sweepout spectrum.

---

### Program theorem 10.3 — Symplectic slicing principle

**Statement.** Let ((M,\omega)) be a symplectic manifold with a compatible metric. Symplectic rigidity should impose lower- or upper-bounds on certain homological slicing spectra, especially those detected by (J)-holomorphic or calibrated cycles.

**Robust content.** Symplectic geometry should have a homological probability theory analogous to the scalar-curvature case, but with symplectic balls, capacities, and compatible metrics replacing ordinary metric balls.

## 11. Quantum and noncommutative probability

### Theorem 11.1 — Quantum probability as a moment map

**Statement.** Classical probabilities arise from quantum amplitudes by the map

[
[z_1:\cdots:z_n]\mapsto
\left(
\frac{|z_1|^2}{\sum_j |z_j|^2},
\dots,
\frac{|z_n|^2}{\sum_j |z_j|^2}
\right).
]

This is the standard moment map

[
\mathbb CP^{n-1}\to \Delta^{n-1}.
]

**Robust content.** Classical probability is a quotient shadow of projective Hilbert-space geometry.

---

### Theorem 11.2 — Von Neumann entropy and strong subadditivity

**Statement.** For a density matrix (\rho),

[
S(\rho)=-\operatorname{Tr}(\rho\log\rho).
]

For a tripartite quantum state (\rho_{ABC}),

[
S(AB)+S(BC)\ge S(B)+S(ABC).
]

**Robust content.** Entropy subadditivity survives in the noncommutative setting, but its proof requires genuinely operator-theoretic inequalities.

## 12. Main proposed theorem-schema

### Program theorem 12.1 — Homological probability principle

**Statement.** Let (X) be a state space, (O) an observable space, and

[
f:X\to O
]

an observation map. Instead of assigning to each event (U\subset O) a number (P(U)\in[0,1]), assign a structured invariant

[
\mathbb P_{\mathrm{hom}}(U)=I(U)\subset H^*(X;F),
]

or more generally an object in a category of modules, ideals, spectra, weighted trees, or filtered homology groups.

The scalar probability is then a decategorified shadow:

[
P(U)=\Phi(\mathbb P_{\mathrm{hom}}(U)),
]

where (\Phi) may be rank, dimension, trace, Euler characteristic, entropy, or asymptotic growth.

**Robust content.** Probability should not always be real-valued. In systems with topology, symmetry, language, or biological correlation, the correct probability object may be categorical, homological, or graph-valued.

## 13. Summary of the robust claims

The lecture’s ideas can be condensed into the following thesis.

### Thesis

Classical probability works well when the relevant observable is scalar and when independence, symmetry, or concentration reduces structure to numbers. But in many natural systems—polymers, proteins, languages, membranes, cycle spaces, quantum systems, and geometric sweepouts—the event itself carries topology or combinatorics. In such cases, probability should be replaced or refined by structured invariants: trees, ideals, filtered homology groups, spectra, or functorial assignments.

### Core theorem pattern

A robust theorem in this framework should have the form

[
\text{real phenomenon}
\longrightarrow
\text{configuration space}
\longrightarrow
\text{filtration}
\longrightarrow
\text{homological invariant}
\longrightarrow
\text{numerical shadow}.
]

The numerical probability is the last step, not the first.
Your proposed principle can be made precise, but I would weaken the last sentence.

Not: **probability requires invertibility**.

Better: **scalar probability behaves well only after the non-invertible directions have been quotiented, projected away, or replaced by structured/homological data.**

Ordinary probability exists in every dimension; there is a large standard theory of high-dimensional probability. For example, van Handel’s notes explicitly develop probability in high dimension as a subject at the interface of probability, analysis, and geometry. So the obstruction is not “probability cannot be defined above dimension (3).” The obstruction is more Gromovian: many natural geometric probability models on cycles, manifolds, membranes, self-avoiding objects, null cones, or configuration spaces stop reducing to scalar probabilities once singular topology and non-invertibility dominate. 

A sharper formulation would be:

```markdown
## Principle — Invertibility obstruction to scalar probability

Scalar probability is stable when the observable map, transfer operator,
incidence pairing, or normalization mechanism has no uncontrolled kernel.

When kernels, null cones, nilpotent sectors, singular strata, or nontrivial
homology appear, scalar probability should be replaced by a structured object:
a conditional probability, a quotient probability, a Drazin-resolved inverse,
a sheaf of local probabilities, or a homological probability.
```

The division-algebra analogy is relevant but only in a restricted sense. The real normed division algebras occur only in dimensions

[
1,;2,;4,;8,
]

corresponding to

[
\mathbb R,\quad \mathbb C,\quad \mathbb H,\quad \mathbb O.
]

Hurwitz’s theorem states that if a bilinear product on (\mathbb R^n) preserves the norm,

[
|x\circ y|=|x|,|y|,
]

then (n=1,2,4,8).  These algebras have no zero divisors in the normed sense: if (xy=0) and (x\ne 0), then (y=0). Equivalently, multiplication by a nonzero element is invertible. That is the algebraic prototype of “no null directions.”

But this does not imply that probability only exists in dimensions (1,2,4,8). It says that certain exceptionally clean multiplicative probability geometries only exist in those dimensions. For example:

[
\mathbb C
]

supports planar conformal probability;

[
\mathbb H
]

is tied to three-dimensional rotations and four-dimensional self-duality;

[
\mathbb O
]

is tied to exceptional seven-dimensional geometry.

So the theorem-bank version should not say “probability requires division algebras.” It should say something like this:

```markdown
## Theorem-schema — Division-algebra regularity

In dimensions where a normed division algebra controls the local geometry,
nonzero multiplication is invertible and null divisors are absent. Therefore
certain probability constructions can be scalarized because the local symmetry
does not create uncontrolled kernels.

Outside the division-algebra dimensions, the same constructions usually acquire
zero divisors, singular strata, nonassociativity, noncanonical choices, or
nontrivial kernels. The probability object then should not be expected to be a
single number.
```

The Drazin inverse fits this picture very well. It is precisely the inverse-like operation for a singular operator with a controlled nilpotent or null sector. If a matrix decomposes into an invertible block and a nilpotent block,

[
A \sim N\oplus C,
]

then the Drazin inverse acts as

[
A^D\sim 0\oplus C^{-1}.
]

So it inverts the invertible part and kills the non-invertible part. Crooks describes this exactly: the Drazin inverse “inverts the invertible part” and zeros the non-invertible part; the associated projector detects the null-space. ([Gavin E. Crooks][1])

This is highly relevant to probability because Markov generators are usually singular. A continuous-time Markov rate matrix has a zero eigenvalue corresponding to the stationary distribution. Therefore it cannot be inverted on the full space. But the Drazin, or group, inverse inverts it on the non-stationary subspace and projects away the stationary null direction. Crooks notes this explicitly for ergodic rate matrices: the zero eigenvalue prevents ordinary inversion, while the Drazin/group inverse resolves the dynamics on the non-null space. ([Gavin E. Crooks][1])

A robust theorem-bank entry could be:

```markdown
## Theorem — Drazin regularization of probabilistic singularity

Let \(A\) be a finite-dimensional evolution, incidence, or transfer operator
appearing in a probability model. Suppose \(A\) has a decomposition

\[
A \sim N \oplus C,
\]

where \(N\) is nilpotent and \(C\) is invertible. Then the Drazin inverse

\[
A^D \sim 0 \oplus C^{-1}
\]

defines the inverse dynamics on the observable non-null sector and annihilates
the invisible or stationary sector.

Thus a singular probabilistic system may still admit a well-defined inverse
after projection away from its null space.
```

The null-cone and light-ray examples should be treated as geometric versions of the same obstruction. In Lorentzian geometry, the equation

[
q(x)=0
]

defines the light cone. On this cone the metric degenerates: null vectors have zero length but are not zero vectors. Therefore the usual Euclidean recipe

[
\text{probability}=\frac{\text{volume of event}}{\text{volume of total space}}
]

breaks down unless one chooses an extra structure: a time slice, gauge fixing, compactification, projectivization, energy shell, or invariant measure class. So the null cone is not a place where probability is impossible; it is a place where scalar normalized probability is not canonical.

A useful formulation:

```markdown
## Principle — Null-cone obstruction

Let \(Q\) be an indefinite quadratic form and let

\[
\mathcal N=\{x:Q(x)=0\}
\]

be its null cone. Since the induced metric on \(\mathcal N\) is degenerate,
there is generally no canonical finite probability measure on \(\mathcal N\)
without extra choices.

Therefore probability on null cones is naturally projective, conditional,
gauge-fixed, or sheaf-like rather than absolutely scalar.
```

The “low-dimensional topological boundary” point also belongs here. In dimensions (2) and (3), boundaries, dualities, and chirality are unusually strong. Planar percolation uses dual curves. Three-dimensional geometry has knots, linking, chirality, and cross-product intuition. In higher dimensions, complementary cycles no longer behave as simple barriers. A codimension-one hypersurface still separates under suitable hypotheses, but higher-codimension cycles generally do not. This is why homological probability becomes natural: the event is not “crossed / not crossed” but “which homology class, ideal, or obstruction survives?”

The corresponding theorem-schema:

```markdown
## Principle — Boundary-to-homology transition

In low dimensions, random events are often detected by visible boundaries:
curves in surfaces, surfaces in three-manifolds, linking, crossing, and
separation.

In higher dimensions, visible boundary events are replaced by homological
events. The probability of an event should therefore take values not merely in
\([0,1]\), but in a graded object such as

\[
H_*(X),\quad H^*(X),\quad \operatorname{Ideals}(H^*(X)),\quad
K(X),\quad \text{or filtered spectra}.
\]
```

So the clean synthesis is:

```markdown
# Invertibility and Homological Probability

## Thesis

Classical scalar probability works best when the observable structure has an
invertible normalization: no uncontrolled kernel, no null cone, no zero
divisors, no singular stratum, and no hidden homology.

When such obstructions appear, probability does not disappear. It changes
type. It becomes conditional, projective, Drazin-regularized, sheaf-valued,

## Scalar regime
```

# Theorem Bank: Probability, Symmetry, Invertibility, and Homological Probability

## Legend

**Theorem** means the statement is standard, tautological from the definitions, or safe under the stated hypotheses.

**Definition** fixes terminology.

**Principle** records a robust mathematical heuristic.

**Program theorem** is a theorem-shaped research target. It should not be cited as proved until the relevant category, topology, convergence, and functoriality hypotheses are specified.

**Conjecture** records a plausible but non-established mathematical claim.

# 0. Meta-principle: mathematics as correct naming

## Principle 0.1 — Poincaré–Grothendieck naming principle

**Statement.** A mathematical theory becomes powerful when it identifies several apparently different phenomena as instances of the same structure and gives that structure the right name.

**Robust content.** The relevant object is not a formula in isolation. The relevant object is the invariant pattern preserved across examples.

**Typical forms.**

A probability model becomes robust when it is functorial under changes of observables.

A geometric model becomes robust when it is stable under perturbation, scaling, degeneration, and passage to a limit.

A biological or linguistic model becomes mathematical when it identifies the invariant combinatorial, categorical, or topological structure behind observed data.

# 1. Probability, invertibility, and division-algebra obstructions

## Theorem 1.1 — Classical probability has no dimension cutoff

**Statement.** Ordinary Kolmogorov probability can be defined on spaces of any dimension. In particular, there is no intrinsic obstruction to defining a probability measure on (\mathbb R^n), on manifolds of arbitrary dimension, or on infinite-dimensional measurable spaces.

**Robust content.** Probability as measure theory does not require dimension (1), (2), (3), (4), or (8). The obstruction appears only when probability is forced to interact with additional algebraic, metric, invertible, or topological structures.

---

## Theorem 1.2 — Event algebras naturally have zero divisors

**Statement.** Let ((\Omega,\mathcal F,P)) be a probability space. Represent events by indicator functions. Then multiplication is intersection:

[
1_A1_B=1_{A\cap B}.
]

If (A,B\neq\varnothing) are disjoint, then

[
1_A1_B=0
]

while neither (1_A) nor (1_B) is zero.

**Conclusion.** The algebra of events usually has zero divisors.

**Robust content.** Classical probability is not division-algebraic. Zero divisors are not a pathology; they encode mutually exclusive events.

---

## Theorem 1.3 — Conditioning is an interior operation

**Statement.** Conditional probability

[
P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)}
]

is defined only when (P(B)>0).

Similarly, likelihood ratios and Radon–Nikodym derivatives require absolute continuity, and logarithmic coordinates require strictly positive densities.

**Robust content.** Probability itself tolerates zero-probability boundaries. But smooth information geometry, conditioning, logarithmic coordinates, and density-ratio calculus require an interior positivity condition.

---

## Theorem 1.4 — Fisher geometry lives on the positive cone

**Statement.** On the interior of the finite probability simplex

[
\Delta^{n-1}_{>0}
=================

\left{p_i>0,\ \sum_i p_i=1\right},
]

the Fisher metric is

[
g=\sum_i \frac{dp_i^2}{p_i}.
]

Under the square-root map

[
p\mapsto 2(\sqrt{p_1},\dots,\sqrt{p_n}),
]

this becomes the round spherical metric on the positive orthant of a sphere.

**Robust content.** The boundary (p_i=0) is a singular boundary for the smooth information geometry, even though it is perfectly legal for measure-theoretic probability.

---

## Theorem 1.5 — Division algebra dimension obstruction

**Statement.** Finite-dimensional real division algebras exist only in dimensions

[
1,\ 2,\ 4,\ 8.
]

The normed real division algebras are, up to isomorphism,

[
\mathbb R,\quad \mathbb C,\quad \mathbb H,\quad \mathbb O.
]

The normed statement is Hurwitz’s theorem; the broader finite-dimensional real division-algebra dimension restriction is the Bott–Milnor–Kervaire (1,2,4,8) phenomenon. ([Wikipedia][1])

**Robust content.** If one demands multiplication, norm compatibility, and invertibility of all nonzero elements, then only exceptional low-dimensional algebraic worlds survive.

---

## Theorem 1.6 — Null cones are non-invertibility boundaries

**Statement.** In a Clifford algebra associated to a quadratic form (Q), a vector (v) satisfies

[
v^2=Q(v).
]

If (Q(v)\neq 0), then (v) is invertible, with inverse proportional to

[
\frac{v}{Q(v)}.
]

If (Q(v)=0), then (v) is null and non-invertible.

**Robust content.** Light cones are algebraic boundaries where inverse formulas blow up. This is the geometric analogue of zero-probability conditioning denominators and singular Fisher metrics.

---

## Principle 1.7 — Drazin inverse as boundary repair, not true invertibility

**Statement.** Generalized inverses such as the Drazin inverse repair part of a singular operator by inverting the invertible spectral block and suppressing the nilpotent or singular block.

**Robust content.** Generalized inverses are useful at singularities, but they do not restore ordinary invertibility. They are algebraic boundary-management devices.

---

## Program theorem 1.8 — Invertible probability is exceptional

**Statement.** A probability theory that requires global invertibility, no zero divisors, normed multiplication, or division-algebraic amplitudes is forced into exceptional low-dimensional algebraic structures.

A probability theory intended for high-dimensional configuration spaces should instead be categorical, measure-theoretic, homological, or functorial.

**Robust content.** Ordinary probability survives in all dimensions because it is not globally invertible. Division-algebraic probability is exceptional.

# 2. Classical probability as the first layer

## Theorem 2.1 — Hardy–Weinberg idempotence

**Statement.** Let (A=(a_{ij})) be a nonnegative matrix with

[
\sum_{i,j}a_{ij}=1.
]

Define row and column marginals

[
r_i=\sum_j a_{ij},\qquad c_j=\sum_i a_{ij}.
]

Define the recombination map

[
T(A)_{ij}=r_ic_j.
]

Then

[
T^2=T.
]

Equivalently, after one random-mating step, the population lies on the rank-one marginal product locus, and further random mating does not change the genotype distribution.

**Robust content.** Mendelian inheritance is not merely a formula. It is an idempotent rational transformation on a simplex of genotype distributions.

**Proof sketch.** The row and column marginals of (T(A)) are again (r_i) and (c_j). Therefore applying (T) again gives the same matrix.

---

## Theorem 2.2 — Law of large numbers as state stabilization

**Statement.** Let (X_1,X_2,\dots) be independent identically distributed random variables with finite expectation. Then

[
\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N X_i\to \mathbb E[X_1]
]

in probability, and under standard hypotheses almost surely.

**Robust content.** Probability becomes physically meaningful not because individual microscopic states are observable, but because aggregate statistics stabilize.

---

## Theorem 2.3 — Entropy as asymptotic state counting

**Statement.** Let (p=(p_1,\dots,p_k)) be a probability vector. The number of length-(N) words with empirical frequencies approximately (p_i) grows as

[
\exp\bigl(NH(p)+o(N)\bigr),
]

where

[
H(p)=-\sum_i p_i\log p_i.
]

**Robust content.** The slogan “entropy is the logarithm of the number of states” becomes mathematically correct only after passing to repeated experiments and using concentration.

---

## Theorem 2.4 — Subadditivity and strong subadditivity of entropy

**Statement.** For finite random variables (X,Y),

[
H(X,Y)\le H(X)+H(Y),
]

with equality precisely when (X) and (Y) are independent.

More generally,

[
H(X,Y,Z)+H(Y)\le H(X,Y)+H(Y,Z).
]

**Robust content.** Correlation decreases the number of independently observable states.

# 3. Kinetic probability and functoriality

## Program theorem 3.1 — Boltzmann naturality principle

**Statement.** A kinetic equation should not be viewed merely as a differential equation attached to one gas. It should be viewed as a natural transformation from a class of microscopic collision systems to a class of macroscopic evolution equations:

[
\text{microscopic collision law}
\longmapsto
\text{kinetic equation}
\longmapsto
\text{hydrodynamic equation}.
]

**Robust content.** The meaningful structure is not one formula. It is the functorial passage between levels of description.

**Caution.** This becomes a theorem only after specifying the relevant categories, morphisms, limiting procedures, and admissible collision laws.

---

## Theorem 3.2 — BBGKY hierarchy

**Statement.** Let (f_N) be the (N)-particle distribution evolving under Liouville dynamics, and let (f_N^{(k)}) be the (k)-particle marginal. Then the marginals satisfy a coupled hierarchy of the form

[
\partial_t f_N^{(k)}+L_k f_N^{(k)}
==================================

C_{k,k+1}f_N^{(k+1)}.
]

Thus the evolution of (k)-particle statistics depends on ((k+1))-particle statistics.

**Robust content.** Marginalization is not closed. Closure requires an additional assumption, typically independence, weak correlation, or propagation of chaos.

---

## Theorem 3.3 — Boltzmann equation as a short-time scaling limit

**Statement.** In the Boltzmann–Grad scaling limit, under suitable chaotic initial data, hard-sphere dynamics converges for short times to the Boltzmann hierarchy; factorized solutions yield the Boltzmann equation.

**Robust content.** The Boltzmann equation is rigorously derivable in a restricted short-time regime. The physically relevant long-time regime is subtler. Lanford’s theorem is the classical result in this direction; modern expositions emphasize both propagation of chaos and the short-time limitation. ([École Normale Supérieure Mathematics][2])

---

## Program theorem 3.4 — Entropy-compatible closure principle

**Statement.** A hydrodynamic closure of a kinetic hierarchy is physically admissible only if it is compatible with entropy monotonicity.

**Robust content.** Formal asymptotic expansion is not enough. The reduced equation must preserve the thermodynamic direction of time.

# 4. Percolation and topology

## Theorem 4.1 — Branching-process threshold on a regular tree

**Statement.** Consider bond percolation on a rooted (d)-ary tree, where each edge is retained independently with probability (p). There is a positive probability of an infinite open path if and only if

[
dp>1.
]

Therefore

[
p_c=\frac1d.
]

For the binary tree,

[
p_c=\frac12.
]

**Robust content.** Percolation on a tree is exactly a Galton–Watson branching process.

---

## Theorem 4.2 — Square-lattice bond percolation threshold

**Statement.** For independent bond percolation on the square lattice (\mathbb Z^2),

[
p_c=\frac12.
]

**Robust content.** The threshold is governed by planar duality, but the full theorem requires probability estimates beyond the visual duality argument.

---

## Theorem 4.3 — Smirnov–Cardy conformal invariance

**Statement.** Critical site percolation on the triangular lattice has conformally invariant scaling limits for crossing probabilities. In particular, crossing probabilities converge to Cardy’s formula. For an equilateral triangle, the limiting probability is linear in the relevant boundary coordinate. ([Université de Genève][3])

**Robust content.** At criticality, a discrete probabilistic model produces a conformal invariant of planar domains.

---

## Conjecture 4.4 — Homological percolation duality

**Statement.** Let (M) be a closed (d)-manifold with a cell decomposition and dual cell decomposition. In a random subcomplex model, the appearance of (k)-dimensional homological connectivity should be coupled to the disappearance of complementary ((d-k-1))-dimensional dual obstructions.

In a self-dual situation, the critical parameter should often occur at

[
p=\frac12.
]

**Robust content.** Percolation thresholds are not only probabilistic. They are controlled by Poincaré duality and the topology of random subcomplexes.

**Status.** The statement is theorem-level in some special models and a guiding principle in others.

# 5. Self-avoiding walks and polymers

## Theorem 5.1 — Existence of the connective constant

**Statement.** Let (c_n) be the number of (n)-step self-avoiding walks from a fixed vertex in a transitive lattice. Then the limit

[
\mu=\lim_{n\to\infty}c_n^{1/n}
]

exists.

**Robust content.** Even when the geometry of typical self-avoiding walks is poorly understood, their exponential counting rate is stable.

---

## Theorem 5.2 — Honeycomb-lattice connective constant

**Statement.** For the hexagonal, or honeycomb, lattice,

[
\mu=\sqrt{2+\sqrt2}.
]

Duminil-Copin and Smirnov proved this using a parafermionic observable satisfying part of the discrete Cauchy–Riemann relations. ([arXiv][4])

**Robust content.** Some self-avoiding walk models possess hidden discrete holomorphic structure strong enough to determine exact constants.

---

## Conjecture 5.3 — Metric exponent for self-avoiding walks

**Statement.** A typical (n)-step self-avoiding walk in (\mathbb R^d) should have diameter of order

[
n^\nu.
]

For (d=2,3), one expects

[
\nu>\frac12,
]

reflecting the fact that self-avoidance forces the walk to spread more than ordinary Brownian motion.

Predicted values include

[
\nu=\frac34 \quad \text{in } d=2,
]

and approximately

[
\nu\approx 0.588 \quad \text{in } d=3.
]

**Robust content.** The hard problem is not merely counting walks. The hard problem is proving concentration of their large-scale geometry.

---

## Definition 5.4 — Configuration-space model of self-avoidance

An (n)-step embedded polymer may be modeled as a point in the high-dimensional configuration space

[
\left{
(x_0,\dots,x_n):
|x_{i+1}-x_i|=1,\
|x_i-x_j|\ge \varepsilon \text{ for } i\ne j
\right}.
]

This is the complement of thickened diagonals.

**Robust content.** Self-avoiding walk theory is probability on a nontrivial configuration space, not merely random walk with a constraint.

# 6. Protein folding as filtered topology

## Definition 6.1 — Energy landscape filtration

Let (\mathcal C) be a configuration space of molecular conformations, and let

[
E:\mathcal C\to\mathbb R

\mathcal C_{\le a}

## Theorem 6.2 — Reachability through sublevel connectivity

**Statement.** A conformation (q) is reachable from a conformation (p) without crossing energy level (a) if and only if (p) and (q) lie in the same connected component of

[
\mathcal C_{\le a}.
]

**Robust content.** Folding barriers are topological features of an energy filtration.

## Program theorem 6.3 — Folding as percolation in configuration space

**Statement.** Protein folding can be modeled as a percolation problem on the filtered configuration space

[
\mathcal C_{\le a}.

As the parameter (a), temperature, pH, solvent condition, or free energy threshold changes, the topology of accessible conformations changes.

**Robust content.** Folding is not merely minimization of energy. It is controlled by connectivity, bottlenecks, sliding contacts, excluded volume, and low-dimensional topology of the configuration space.

**Dimension note.** Dimension (3) is special because embedded curves, linking, steric obstruction, and knotting become simultaneously nontrivial. Dimension (2) is too restrictive; higher dimensions loosen self-avoidance and linking constraints.

# 7. Probability in language

## Definition 7.1 — Context tree of a string

Given a corpus and a finite string (w), define the context tree (T(w)) whose branches record extensions of (w) to the left and right, with edge weights given by empirical frequencies.

The scalar frequency of (w) is only the root weight. The tree records how (w) occurs.

---

## Theorem 7.2 — Scalar frequency is not a linguistic probability invariant

**Statement.** Two strings can have the same empirical frequency but non-isomorphic context trees. Therefore scalar frequency alone cannot distinguish their linguistic behavior.

**Robust content.** In language, probability should often take values in weighted trees or graphs, not only in real numbers.

---

## Theorem 7.3 — Winograd obstruction to syntax-only inference

**Statement.** Suppose two sentences have the same syntactic form but require different pronoun resolutions because of lexical or semantic content. Then any model depending only on syntactic form must assign the same resolution to both and therefore fails on at least one.

**Example.**

[
\text{The object does not fit in the container because it is too large.}
]

versus

[
\text{The object does not fit in the container because it is too small.}
]

**Robust content.** Some linguistic probabilities are semantic-contextual invariants, not syntactic frequencies.

---

## Program theorem 7.4 — Language probability as a functor

**Statement.** A probability theory for language should be a functor

[
\mathsf{Language}\longrightarrow \mathsf{WeightedGraphs}
]

or

[
\mathsf{Language}\longrightarrow \mathsf{ContextTrees},
]

rather than only a map to ([0,1]).

**Robust content.** The probability of a sentence is not merely how often it appears. It is the structured distribution of its contexts, continuations, substitutions, and semantic roles.

# 8. Homological probability

## Definition 8.1 — Observable map

Let (X) be a state space and (O) an observable space. An observation is modeled by a map

[
f:X\to O.
]

For an observable event (U\subset O), the corresponding state-space event is

[
X_U=f^{-1}(U).
]

---

## Definition 8.2 — Cohomological support ideal

Let (H^*(X;F)) be cohomology with coefficients in a field (F). Define

[
I(U)
====

\ker\left(
H^*(X;F)\to H^*(X\setminus X_U;F)
\right).
]

Thus (I(U)) consists of cohomology classes supported inside the event (X_U).

---

## Theorem 8.3 — Support ideals behave like generalized measures

**Statement.** The assignment

[
U\mapsto I(U)
]

has the following properties.

If (U\subset V), then

[
I(U)\subset I(V).
]

If (U,V\subset O), then

[
I(U)\cdot I(V)\subset I(U\cap V),
]

where the product is the cup product in (H^*(X;F)).

For sufficiently separated or excisive disjoint events, cohomology with support decomposes additively.

**Robust content.** A homological probability does not assign a number to an event. It assigns an ideal, subspace, module, or algebraic object measuring which global topological features are forced to live over that event.

---

## Definition 8.4 — Homological probability

A homological probability theory is an assignment

[
U\longmapsto I(U)
]

from observable events to algebraic invariants such as subspaces, ideals, modules, spectra, or filtered homology groups, satisfying monotonicity, functoriality, and multiplicative compatibility.

---

## Theorem 8.5 — Scalar probability as a shadow

**Statement.** Given a homological probability (U\mapsto I(U)), scalar invariants may be obtained by applying rank, dimension, Euler characteristic, Poincaré polynomial, trace, or asymptotic logarithmic growth:

[
U\mapsto \dim I(U),
]

[
U\mapsto P_{I(U)}(t),
]

[
U\mapsto
\lim_{N\to\infty}
\frac1N\log \dim I_N(U).
]

**Robust content.** Ordinary probability and entropy can be interpreted as numerical shadows of richer functorial structures.

# 9. Cycle spaces and homological spectra

## Theorem 9.1 — Almgren cycle-space theorem

**Statement.** Let (\mathcal Z_k(M;G)) be the space of (k)-cycles in a manifold (M) with coefficients in an abelian group (G). Under the standard topology on flat cycles,

[
\pi_i(\mathcal Z_k(M;G))
\cong
H_{i+k}(M;G).
]

This is Almgren’s isomorphism theorem, a generalization of the Dold–Thom theorem. ([Wikipedia][5])

**Robust content.** The topology of the space of cycles is controlled by the homology of the underlying manifold.

---

## Definition 9.2 — Volume filtration on cycle space

Let

[
\mathcal Z_k^{\le V}(M)
=======================

{Z\in \mathcal Z_k(M):\operatorname{Vol}_k(Z)\le V}.
]

The inclusions

[
\mathcal Z_k^{\le V}(M)
\hookrightarrow
\mathcal Z_k(M)
]

produce a filtration of the cycle space by volume.

---

## Definition 9.3 — Homological spectrum of cycles

For a homology or cohomology class (\alpha) of the cycle space, define its spectral value by

[
\lambda(\alpha)
===============

\inf\left{
V:\alpha \text{ is detected in } \mathcal Z_k^{\le V}(M)
\right}.
]

The collection of all (\lambda(\alpha)) forms a homological spectrum.

**Robust content.** Eigenvalues of operators are only the first example of a more general phenomenon: topology appearing at critical values of a filtration.

---

## Theorem 9.4 — Weyl law for the volume spectrum

**Statement.** For hypersurface sweepouts in a closed Riemannian manifold (M^D), the (p)-th width (\omega_p(M)) satisfies a Weyl-type asymptotic law

[
\omega_p(M)
\sim
a_D\operatorname{Vol}(M)^{(D-1)/D}p^{1/D},
]

where (a_D) is a universal constant depending only on the dimension. This confirms a conjecture of Gromov for the volume spectrum. ([University of Chicago Mathematics][6])

**Robust content.** Minimal hypersurface spectra behave like eigenvalue spectra: high-index objects distribute according to volume.

---

## Theorem 9.5 — Rayleigh quotient as topological spectrum

**Statement.** Let (A) be a Hermitian matrix with distinct eigenvalues. The Rayleigh quotient

[
R_A([z])
========

\frac{\langle Az,z\rangle}{\langle z,z\rangle}
]

on complex projective space (\mathbb CP^{n-1}) is a Morse function. Its critical values are exactly the eigenvalues of (A). The topology of sublevel sets changes precisely at these eigenvalues.

**Robust content.** Linear-algebraic spectrum can be recovered as the critical-value spectrum of a topological filtration.

# 10. Waist, packing, and covering

## Theorem 10.1 — Gromov waist theorem for the sphere

**Statement.** Let

[
f:S^n\to\mathbb R^k

be continuous. Then there exists (y\in\mathbb R^k) such that, for every (\varepsilon>0), the (\varepsilon)-neighborhood of the fiber (f^{-1}(y)) has volume at least the (\varepsilon)-neighborhood of the equatorial sphere (S^{n-k}\subset S^n). ([IHES][7])

**Robust content.** Every map from a sphere to a lower-dimensional space has at least one large fiber.

**Statement.** Under suitable regularity assumptions, one expects a fiber satisfying

[
\mathcal H^{n-k}(f^{-1}(y))
\ge
\operatorname{Vol}(S^{n-k}).

**Robust content.** The proven waist theorem controls neighborhoods, or Minkowski-type content. A direct Hausdorff-measure version is subtler.

---

## Program theorem 10.3 — Parametric packing principle

**Statement.** Let (C_n^r(M)) be the configuration space of (n) disjoint radius-(r) balls in a metric manifold (M). Homology classes in (C_n^r(M)) detect not only whether balls fit, but how they can move.

**Robust content.** Packing is not merely a scalar optimization problem. It has a homological refinement: the topology of the configuration space records movable packings.

# 13. Main theorem schema

## Program theorem 13.1 — Homological probability principle

# 14. Final synthesis

## Thesis 14.1 — Scalar probability is the first shadow, not the whole object

Classical probability works well when the relevant observable is scalar and when independence, symmetry, exchangeability, or concentration reduces structure to numbers.

But in many natural systems—polymers, proteins, languages, membranes, random complexes, quantum systems, geometric sweepouts, and configuration spaces—the event itself carries topology or combinatorics. In such cases, probability should be refined by structured invariants:

[
\text{weighted trees},
\quad
\text{ideals},
\quad
\text{filtered homology groups},
\quad
\text{spectra},
\quad
\text{functorial assignments}.
]

The numerical probability is then obtained only after applying a decategorifying functional.

## Thesis 14.2 — Invertibility is local; probability is global

Probability does not require global invertibility. Classical event algebras have zero divisors, Markov kernels lose information, conditioning fails on zero-probability events, and coarse-graining is irreversible.

Invertibility appears only on special interiors:

[

\qquad

\qquad
Q(v)\neq0,
\qquad
\det A\neq0.
]

The boundary of probability is therefore analogous to a null cone, singular variety, or non-invertible stratum.
