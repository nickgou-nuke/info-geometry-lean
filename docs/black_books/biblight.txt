The (J)-mirror should first be formalized as a **Pin reflective boundary**, not immediately as a D-brane. It becomes D-brane-like only after you add localized boundary degrees of freedom, boundary charge, and mode anchoring. Structurally, the raw (J)-mirror is closer to an **orientifold-like reflection plane**: it implements a quotient/reflection, has a fixed boundary locus, reverses orientation/chirality, and imposes a projection law. In string theory, orientifold planes are produced by gauging a discrete symmetry involving worldsheet parity/reflection, and they are described as fixed loci of an involution; D-branes are dynamical charged boundary objects that can carry open-string/gauge degrees of freedom. ([www-library.desy.de][1])

So the clean hierarchy is:

```text
Pin reflection:
    geometric reflection / chirality flip / V₄ component jump

Tomita J-mirror:
    operator-algebraic realization of that reflection

Orientifold-like boundary:
    fixed-locus / quotient / reflection projection

D-brane-like boundary:
    reflection boundary plus localized modes, charge, and anchoring

Cosmological resonator wall:
    Pin/Tomita reflective boundary supporting Casimir shells and charge balance
```

The reason (Pin) is mandatory is exactly as stated: (Spin) covers the connected (SO)-type rotation/boost sector, while (Pin) covers the full orthogonal group including reflections. In Clifford terms, (Spin) is the even part, while (Pin) also includes odd Clifford products; a degree-one Pin element acts as a reflection. ([Wikipedia][2])

For (O(p,q)) with (p,q>0), the real disconnected components are not visible to the Lie algebra. The Lie algebra only sees the infinitesimal connected part; the (P,T,PT) sectors are global group data. This is why perturbative QFT can ignore them for many scattering calculations, but boundary, anomaly, and nonorientable problems force them back in. This is also exactly why Pin structures appear in fermionic topological phases and reflection-positive/invertible phase classifications. Freed–Hopkins explicitly frame interacting topological phases through reflection positivity and bordism data, which is the modern mathematical home of these reflection-sensitive structures. ([arXiv][3])

One correction to keep the claim honest: the use of complex Hilbert spaces is not by itself “wrong,” and it does not invalidate standard QFT. CPT, parity, and time reversal are handled successfully there as unitary or antiunitary symmetries. The issue is that complexification often hides the real Clifford signature and the disconnected (O/Pin) topology. For bulk perturbative calculations that is usually harmless. For your resonator-boundary problem, it is not harmless, because the boundary is precisely where reflection, chirality flip, commutant routing, and Pin signs matter.

So the answer to the “wrong covering group” question is:

[
\boxed{
\text{Using only }Spin/SO\text{ is not the whole cause of the QFT/GR problem, but it is a real structural blind spot whenever reflections, boundaries, Majorana reality, nonorientability, or topological phases are essential.}
}
