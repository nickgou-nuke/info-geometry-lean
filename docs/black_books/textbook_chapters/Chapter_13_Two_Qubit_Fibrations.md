# Section 13: Fibrations and the Structure of Two-Qubit Space

The seemingly abstract space of two-qubit states can be better understood by employing the mathematical concept of fibrations. A fibration is a structure where a total space is mapped onto a base space, with each point in the base space corresponding to a fiber. While the Hopf fibration relevant to two qubits is more complex, a simple analogy is a cylinder: the cylinder is the total space, a circle around the cylinder is the base space, and a line running the height of the cylinder (at a fixed point on the circle) is the fiber. This analogy helps illustrate the general idea of a fibration, but the Hopf fibration involves higher-dimensional spheres and a more intricate "twisting" of the fibers.

## 13.1 The Hopf Fibration ($S^7 \to S^4$ with fiber $S^3$)

The relevant fibration for understanding two-qubit states is a specific type of Hopf fibration. This fibration reveals a decomposition of the 7-sphere representing all two-qubit states into a 4-dimensional base space (related to $S^4$) representing entanglement classes between the qubits, and a 3-dimensional fiber (related to $S^3$) representing local unitary transformations on the individual qubits (modulo a global phase).

*   **$S^7$ (7-sphere):** The space of all normalized two-qubit states is topologically a 7-sphere. A two-qubit state is a vector in a 4-dimensional complex vector space, $\mathbb{C}^4$. Normalization (requiring the sum of squared magnitudes of the amplitudes to be 1) restricts the states to the unit sphere in $\mathbb{C}^4$, which is the $S^7$ sphere.
*   **$S^4$ (Base Space related to 4-sphere):** This base space, while often referred to as $S^4$ for simplicity, is more precisely related to the space of entanglement classes of two-qubit states under local unitary transformations. The Hopf fibration maps the 7-sphere of two-qubit states onto this 4-dimensional space in a way that separates entanglement properties from local unitary degrees of freedom.
*   **$S^3$ (Fiber related to 3-sphere):** The fiber of this fibration is topologically a 3-sphere (related to $S^3$). More accurately, the fiber is related to the group of local unitary transformations $SU(2) \times U(1)$ (or a quotient thereof), which acts on each qubit independently, along with a relative phase. One might initially think of two qubits as each having a Bloch sphere ($S^2$), leading to $S^2 \times S^2$. However, the local unitary transformations on a single qubit are described by $SU(2)$, which is topologically $S^3$. The structure of local unitary operations on two qubits, plus the fibration structure, gives rise to this $S^3$-related fiber.

**Separable States:** Separable (non-entangled) two-qubit states correspond to a specific submanifold within $S^7$. Under the Hopf map, these separable states are mapped to a particular region within the 4-dimensional base space, representing the class of states with zero entanglement. It is not that the $S^4$ collapses to a point, but rather separable states are mapped to a specific, lower-dimensional subspace within the 4-dimensional base space.

**Entangled States:** Different entanglement classes of two-qubit states are mapped to different regions within the 4-dimensional base space. States with higher entanglement are mapped to regions conceptually "further" from the region corresponding to separable states in this 4-dimensional space. This 4-dimensional base space thus serves to parameterize and classify the entanglement properties of two-qubit states.

**Visualizing (Higher Dimensional Fibration):** Imagine a fibration as a "bundle," where over each "point" in the 4-dimensional base space, there is a 3-dimensional "fiber." In the Hopf fibration, for each entanglement class (represented by a "point" in the 4-dimensional base space), there is a set of local unitary transformations (represented by the 3-dimensional fiber) that can be applied to reach different physical states within that entanglement class. Note that visualizing this fibration directly in 3D is impossible, and the "twisting" and bundling are topological features in higher dimensions, not simple geometric twists in 3D space.

## 13.2 Connection to the Isomorphism and Gamma Matrices

The 4-dimensional base space (related to $S^4$) in the Hopf fibration is closely related to the 4D Euclidean space we discussed earlier in the context of the isomorphism and gamma matrices. The gamma matrices, through the Horodecki representation, provide a way to parameterize this 4-dimensional space and to quantify and classify entanglement. Specifically, coordinates in this 4-dimensional space can be constructed from expectation values of bilinear combinations of qubit operators involving gamma matrices.

These combinations are directly related to entanglement measures and allow for a geometric representation of entanglement types. The coordinates on the base space (related to $S^4$) can be expressed in terms of these expectation values, providing a direct link between the geometric picture of the fibration and the algebraic description using the Dirac matrices and the Horodecki representation.

## Conclusion

Incorporating the concept of fibrations, particularly the Hopf fibration, provides a powerful and elegant way to understand the structure of the two-qubit state space, especially regarding the classification of entanglement classes. It reveals how the 7-dimensional space of two-qubit states can be decomposed into a 4-dimensional space parameterizing entanglement types and degrees, and a 3-dimensional space related to local unitary transformations.

The connection to the 4D space represented by the gamma matrices through the isomorphism and Horodecki representation further strengthens this picture. It adds a geometric layer to the algebraic description, providing a richer and more intuitive understanding of the quantum information geometry of two-qubit systems and their relation to spacetime and entanglement.
